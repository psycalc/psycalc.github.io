Compatibility studies: prediction and relationship interventions

What these eight studies address #

Predicting an initial attraction, describing satisfaction, forecasting separation, and testing an intervention are different scientific tasks. These eight peer-reviewed publications illustrate those differences. They concern romantic relationships; transfer to friendship or teamwork requires new evidence. None validates BWB typologies. Intervention findings are research summaries, not individual treatment recommendations.

Start with Catalog of Scientific Research on Compatibility or return to A History of Attempts to Solve Human Compatibility. Search and access details: research/compatibility-catalog/prediction-search.md.

Joel, Eastwick and Finkel (2017): predicting a spark #

Source: Samantha Joel, Paul W. Eastwick and Eli J. Finkel. Is Romantic Desire Predictable? Machine Learning Applied to Initial Romantic Attraction. Psychological Science, 28(10), 1478–1489. DOI and journal abstract.

Question and design: Can questionnaires taken before meeting predict who will desire whom? Two speed-dating studies used more than 100 self-report measures and four-minute meetings between opposite-sex participants. The verified abstract does not give the participant count; it is not reconstructed here.

Finding in plain language: Models could partly identify people who tended to desire others and people who tended to attract interest. They could not predict the additional attraction unique to a particular pairing from the available questionnaires. General popularity and a specific spark are different outcomes.

Limits: This result concerns these measures, models and brief encounters. It establishes neither permanent unpredictability nor the impossibility of learning from subsequent interaction. Access: abstract.

BWB implication: Any future prediction study should distinguish general actor and partner tendencies from a pair-specific contribution, then test that contribution on unseen data. This is a proposed methodological baseline, not typological validation.

Joel and colleagues (2020): satisfaction is easier than change #

Source: Samantha Joel, Paul W. Eastwick and colleagues. Machine learning uncovers the most robust self-report predictors of relationship quality across 43 longitudinal couples studies. PNAS, 117(32), 19061–19071. DOI; author-hosted full text.

Question and design: Which reports best predict relationship quality? Machine-learning analyses combined 43 longitudinal datasets covering 11,196 couples, with follow-up ranging from two to 48 months.

Finding in plain language: People's reports about their existing relationship were more informative than individual characteristics. Perceived partner commitment, appreciation, sexual satisfaction, perceived partner satisfaction and conflict stood out. However, predicting whose satisfaction would improve or decline remained largely unsuccessful. Knowing how things feel now differs from forecasting their direction.

Limits: These are observational self-reports from established couples. Shared reporting and overlapping meanings can strengthen associations. The findings do not establish causal priorities or a method for matching strangers. Access: full text.

BWB implication: A future study should compare proposed constructs with relationship-specific reports and separately evaluate present level, later level and change. These are different endpoints; success on one cannot substitute for validation on another.

Finkel and colleagues (2012): access is not matching accuracy #

Source: Eli J. Finkel, Paul W. Eastwick, Benjamin R. Karney, Harry T. Reis and Susan Sprecher. Online Dating: A Critical Analysis From the Perspective of Psychological Science. Psychological Science in the Public Interest, 13(1), 3–66. DOI; author-hosted full text.

Question and design: Does online dating improve romantic outcomes? This critical narrative review separates access to people, online communication and algorithmic matching. It has no single participant sample or pooled experimental effect.

Finding in plain language: A service can help people meet without demonstrating that its algorithm selects unusually successful pairs. The authors found no compelling evidence, at the time, for commercial claims of superior matching outcomes. Profiles also leave much of real interaction unobserved.

Limits: This is an assessment of evidence available in 2012, not a test of every later platform. Absence of convincing evidence then is not proof that all future algorithms must fail. Access: full text.

BWB implication: Evaluate whether a conversation tool helps its users separately from any claim about selecting partners. A useful interface or a persuasive explanation cannot itself establish prediction accuracy, and this review supplies no validation of BWB's proposed type patterns.

Gottman and Levenson (2000): different routes to divorce #

Source: John Mordechai Gottman and Robert Wayne Levenson. The Timing of Divorce: Predicting When a Couple Will Divorce Over a 14-Year Period. Journal of Marriage and Family, 62(3), 737–745. DOI; author-hosted article.

Question and design: Do the same interaction patterns anticipate earlier and later divorce? An observational cohort of 79 married couples was followed for 14 years; 22 divorced. Researchers coded conflict and daily-events conversations and considered relationship reports.

Finding in plain language: Negative emotion during conflict was associated with earlier divorce, whereas scarce positive emotion was associated with later divorce. A quiet conversation and an affectionate conversation need not mean the same thing.

Limits: The cohort and event count were small. The reported 93% classification accuracy is a result within this study, not an established success rate for new couples. It does not authorize an individual divorce forecast. Access: journal abstract and full-article excerpts; PDF extraction was incomplete.

BWB implication: Record everyday connection as well as conflict, specify the forecast horizon, and require external validation. These methodological lessons do not confirm a typological mechanism.

Kim, Capaldi and Crosby (2007): a generalizability test failed #

Source: Hyoun K. Kim, Deborah M. Capaldi and Lynn Crosby. Generalizability of Gottman and Colleagues' Affective Process Models of Couples' Relationship Outcomes. Journal of Marriage and Family, 69(1), 55–72. DOI; PubMed abstract.

Question and design: Would affective-process predictions from Gottman and colleagues' 1998 study generalize? Researchers studied 85 married or cohabiting couples with at-risk backgrounds, assessing interaction around age 21 and relationship outcomes about 2.5 years later.

Finding in plain language: Major predictions did not replicate, including several proposed gender-specific pathways to separation. Discussion patterns also differed according to whose topic the couple discussed. An observation protocol can influence the very behavior being measured.

Limits: This tests generalizability across a different population and task context. It neither refutes every finding about couple communication nor directly replicates the 2000 timing study above. Access: abstract; the PMC full-text route returned a browser challenge.

BWB implication: Preserve unsuccessful tests alongside positive findings, vary discussion topics deliberately, and check portability across populations. A compelling narrative about a couple is not sufficient evidence that its proposed predictive mechanism travels to another setting.

Roddy and colleagues (2020): change after couple therapy #

Source: McKenzie K. Roddy, Lucia M. Walsh, Karen Rothman, S. Gabe Hatch and Brian D. Doss. Meta-analysis of couple therapy: Effects across outcomes, designs, timeframes, and other moderators. Journal of Consulting and Clinical Psychology, 88(7), 583–596. DOI; PubMed abstract.

Question and design: What changes follow couple therapy across different studies? A random-effects meta-analysis covered 58 studies representing 40 distinct samples and 2,092 couples.

Finding in plain language: Relationship satisfaction improved substantially within treatment groups; the reported pre-to-post effect was Hedges' g = 1.12. Waitlist groups showed little average change. Communication and emotional intimacy also improved. People experiencing relationship distress therefore need not remain at the same measured level forever.

Limits: The headline effect is a within-group change, not the pooled difference against a randomized active treatment. The samples were exclusively opposite-sex couples, and dependencies between results could not be fully modeled. Access: abstract.

BWB implication: Treat relationship outcomes as potentially changeable and record interventions during follow-up. Improvement after established therapy does not demonstrate that BWB conversations, automated guidance or typological interpretation will produce the same effects. This summary does not recommend a treatment for any particular couple.

Hawkins and colleagues (2008): relationship education #

Source: Alan J. Hawkins, Victoria L. Blanchard, Scott A. Baldwin and Elizabeth B. Fawcett. Does marriage and relationship education work? A meta-analytic study. Journal of Consulting and Clinical Psychology, 76(5), 723–734. DOI; PubMed abstract.

Question and design: Do educational programs improve relationship quality and communication? The meta-analysis synthesized 117 studies from 86 reports, yielding more than 500 effect sizes.

Finding in plain language: Experimental studies showed average benefits: relationship-quality effects ranged from d = .30 to .36 and communication effects from .43 to .45. Teaching interaction skills can change measured outcomes; a larger change in communication does not automatically mean a similarly large change in relationship quality.

Limits: Limited racial, ethnic and economic diversity constrained conclusions for disadvantaged couples. Stability and aggression were rarely measured. Some publication differences appeared for communication at follow-up. Access: abstract. The reported ranges are standardized effects, not percentages of couples helped.

BWB implication: Evaluate conversation skills and relationship outcomes separately, including follow-up rather than immediate impressions alone. This is a baseline for designing research on educational support, not evidence that BWB has achieved comparable benefits or that preserving a relationship is always a safe outcome.

Hawkins and colleagues (2022): smaller effects in a policy program #

Source: Alan J. Hawkins, Sarah Hokanson, Eden Loveridge, Emily Milius, Misha Duncan, McCall Booth and Brittany Pollard. How effective are ACF-funded couple relationship education programs? A meta-analytic study. Family Process, 61(3), 970–985. DOI; PubMed abstract.

Question and design: What did US federally supported relationship-education programs achieve? The meta-analysis examined 32 control-group studies and, separately, 19 single-group before-and-after studies in a policy initiative serving lower-income participants.

Finding in plain language: Controlled effects were small for relationship quality (d = .114) and skills (.132). Effects on relationship stability, parenting and child well-being were not statistically significant. Before-and-after studies gave larger estimates. Feeling better after a program is therefore insufficient to show how much the program itself contributed.

Limits: Outcomes and studies were heterogeneous; the findings concern this program setting. A nonsignificant pooled effect does not prove exactly zero benefit for every subgroup. Access: abstract.

BWB implication: Compare any intervention with an appropriate control and report small or null effects openly. This paper is not a direct replication of the 2008 review, and differences between their estimates cannot be assigned to one cause without additional analysis.

What this evidence can support #

BWB accepts these publications as external research on distinct, context-specific outcomes. They offer baseline variables and design cautions: initial desire, actor and partner effects, satisfaction, commitment, observed interaction, relationship duration and intervention exposure. Associations, within-group improvements and controlled effects must remain separate. The papers do not test BWB's four-level architecture or validate Socionics, Psychosophy or Temporistics. Their evidence also cannot establish moral worth, consent or safety from a profile.

Attributed conclusions #

The cards distinguish source findings from BWB research proposals. Positive predictions, unsuccessful generalizability tests and intervention effects concern different outcomes.

Evidence supplied #

Observational studies, a critical narrative review, a multi-dataset longitudinal analysis and three meta-analyses are included. Sample sizes and effects are reported where verified; access depth is stated per card.

Limits of the collection #

This selection is not an exhaustive systematic review. Full methods were not checked for every paper. Intervention reviews may overlap in included studies and are not independent replications.

What BWB accepts #

Use the papers to define outcome-specific baselines and questions, preserving the difference between description, prediction and intervention.

What remains open #

Portability across populations, incremental prediction by BWB constructs and effectiveness of BWB conversation tools remain untested here.

Unsupported extensions #

Reject interpreting the papers as typology validation, a personal prognosis or a universal pair score. The 2012 platform assessment is historically bounded.

Research route #

Use the linked publications and access record to check each claim. Priority provenance checks concern full methods for abstract-only entries, external validation of the 2000 divorce model, overlap between intervention reviews, and population coverage. No independent provenance review is claimed here. Continue with Catalog of Scientific Research on Compatibility.
