Compatibility studies: teams and friendship

What this collection covers #

Eight selected peer-reviewed papers examine working together and developing friendship. Performance, satisfaction, and emotional closeness are different outcomes. These papers provide comparison variables and alternative explanations for BWB; none validates its typologies. This is a starting collection, not an exhaustive or systematic review. The search and access record is in research/compatibility-catalog/cooperation-search.md.

Start with Catalog of Scientific Research on Compatibility; for the historical context, see A History of Attempts to Solve Human Compatibility. Each final paragraph below is a proposed BWB research use, separate from the authors' findings.

De Dreu and Weingart (2003): disagreement and conflict #

Access checked: full text.

Source: C. K. W. De Dreu and L. R. Weingart. Task versus relationship conflict, team performance, and team member satisfaction: A meta-analysis. Journal of Applied Psychology, 88(4), 741–749. DOI and publication.

Question and design: Is disagreement about work beneficial? This meta-analysis identified 30 eligible studies; the number contributing to each comparison differed. Conflict was measured, not experimentally assigned.

Finding in plain language: Both task conflict and relationship conflict were negatively associated with performance and satisfaction on average. Calling a dispute “about the task” does not automatically make it productive.

Limits: These correlations cannot determine whether conflict worsened performance or disappointing performance produced conflict. Different tasks and measures also limit a universal conclusion about disagreement.

For BWB: Record the subject of a disagreement, personal tension, and the actual work result separately. Ask what happened before and after a particular dispute. This offers a testable comparison against a type-based explanation; it supplies neither a ban on disagreement nor a rule for selecting partners. Examine separate episodes, allowing that the same pair may interact differently under different conditions.

Peeters and colleagues (2006): personality composition #

Access checked: full text.

Source: M. A. G. Peeters, H. F. J. M. van Tuijl, C. G. Rutte, and I. M. M. J. Reymen. Personality and team performance: A meta-analysis. European Journal of Personality, 20, 377–396. DOI and publication.

Question and design: How do team members' Big Five characteristics relate to performance? Depending on the analysis, this meta-analysis covered 106–527 teams, distinguishing average trait levels from variation within a team.

Finding in plain language: Higher average agreeableness and conscientiousness, and less variation in these characteristics, were associated with better performance. “What is the group's average?” and “How different are its members?” therefore require separate questions.

Limits: The evidence base was small, and student and professional teams differed in tasks and time together. Correlations cannot establish a universally optimal composition.

For BWB: Compare any proposed contribution of type patterns against measured traits, experience, and task demands. Specify how individual data become a team variable. The paper does not justify excluding a person from work or predicting a particular pair's success from a personality profile. Such a prediction would require a separate test on new data.

DeChurch and Mesmer-Magnus (2010): coordinating knowledge #

Access checked: full text.

Source: L. A. DeChurch and J. R. Mesmer-Magnus. The cognitive underpinnings of effective teamwork: A meta-analysis. Journal of Applied Psychology, 95(1), 32–53. DOI and publication.

Question and design: Does the way a team organizes knowledge relate to its work? The meta-analysis combined 65 independent studies, 231 correlations, and 3,738 groups.

Finding in plain language: Team cognition was positively associated with coordination and performance. The literature includes shared understandings and knowing how members' different expertise fits together. Their relationships with outcomes varied by measurement and team context.

Limits: A pooled association does not identify the direction of causation. Different definitions of cognition cannot simply be treated as measurements of one identical mechanism.

For BWB: Ask whether partners understand the task, know who holds relevant information, and revise plans when information changes. Observe these processes during actual work. Similar vocabulary about “information models” is insufficient to equate this literature with Socionics: connecting the constructs would require explicit definitions and independent tests. Specify beforehand which observation counts as knowledge coordination and which is merely matching wording.

De Jong and colleagues (2016): trust within teams #

Access checked: abstract.

Source: B. A. de Jong, K. T. Dirks, and N. Gillespie. Trust and team performance: A meta-analysis of main effects, moderators, and covariates. Journal of Applied Psychology, 101(8), 1134–1150. DOI and publication.

Question and design: How strongly does trust between teammates relate to performance? The meta-analysis combined 112 independent studies covering 7,763 teams.

Finding in plain language: Trust and performance were positively related; the corrected correlation was .30. The association persisted in analyses accounting for prior performance and trust in the leader. Task interdependence and differences in authority and skills mattered to its strength.

Limits: The card relies on the authors' institutional abstract. Statistical adjustment does not prove that increasing trust alone causes improvement, and the coefficient is not an individual prediction.

For BWB: Include trust as a comparison variable alongside observable reliability: keeping agreements, communicating errors, and seeking help. Ask which responsibilities require mutual reliance. Trust ratings cannot replace evidence about consent or safe conduct, and they cannot establish that a type pairing is compatible. Partners can clarify the concrete actions on which their trust in each other rests.

McEwan and colleagues (2017): training cooperation #

Access checked: full text.

Source: D. McEwan, G. R. Ruissen, M. A. Eys, B. D. Zumbo, and M. R. Beauchamp. The effectiveness of teamwork training on teamwork behaviors and team performance: A systematic review and meta-analysis of controlled interventions. PLOS ONE, 12(1), e0169604. Open publication.

Question and design: Can training improve teamwork? The review included 51 articles, 72 controlled interventions, and 8,439 participants; it synthesized changes in teamwork and performance.

Finding in plain language: Training improved both outcomes on average. Effects were medium-sized after outlier studies were removed. How people work together can therefore be studied as something that changes with practice.

Limits: Interventions and settings varied substantially. Having a control group does not make all included studies equally strong or establish durable benefits for every training program.

For BWB: A training condition is a useful comparator for any future conversation-map intervention. Measure specific behavior before and after, with a comparison group and later follow-up. Changes following practice should be considered before explaining persistent friction through type patterns. Consider the possibility of learning other ways to work together.

Selfhout and colleagues (2009): feeling similar #

Access checked: abstract.

Source: M. Selfhout, J. Denissen, S. Branje, and W. Meeus. In the eye of the beholder: Perceived, actual, and peer-rated similarity in personality, communication, and friendship intensity during the acquaintanceship process. Journal of Personality and Social Psychology, 96(6), 1152–1165. DOI and publication.

Question and design: Which kind of similarity accompanies friendship formation? Undergraduates, mean age 18.9, provided self- and peer-ratings at five points during their first university year. The accessed abstract does not state sample size.

Finding in plain language: For new acquaintances, perceived and peer-rated similarity were associated with friendship intensity; actual similarity was not. Feeling alike and matching questionnaire profiles need separate measurement.

Limits: This observational university study cannot establish a universal rule about similarity or show that perceptions cause closeness. This card is abstract-based.

For BWB: Collect each person's impression of similarity separately from independently measured characteristics and observed interaction. A feeling that “our types explain us” may reflect expectations or growing closeness. Ask for specific examples and changes over time before treating that feeling as evidence for a process hypothesis.

Roberts and Dunbar (2015): maintaining friendship #

Access checked: full text.

Source: S. B. G. Roberts and R. I. M. Dunbar. Managing relationship decay: Network, gender, and contextual effects. Human Nature, 26(4), 426–450. Open publication.

Question and design: What happens to existing ties during a major life transition? This longitudinal study followed personal networks across 18 months. Thirty young people began; 25 completed all three waves.

Finding in plain language: Greater investment in friendship was associated with less decline in closeness. Reported patterns differed by gender: contact frequency was more protective for women and shared activities for men in this sample.

Limits: The very small cohort and particular transition restrict generalization, especially of gender differences. Observational reports cannot establish that a prescribed amount or form of contact preserves a friendship.

For BWB: Ask about available time, distance, competing relationships, and preferred ways of staying in touch. These contextual explanations should be checked when a formerly close pair drifts apart. Use individual preferences rather than gender rules, and distinguish inability to meet often from unwillingness to sustain the relationship. Partners may understand sufficient attention differently; discuss this directly using examples both understand.

Hall (2019): time for a new friendship #

Access checked: full text.

Source: J. A. Hall. How many hours does it take to make a friend? Journal of Social and Personal Relationships, 36(4), 1278–1296; first published online in 2018. DOI and publication.

Question and design: How are time together and closeness related? Study 1 surveyed 355 recently relocated adults. Study 2 followed 112 first-year students reporting on two acquaintances three times over nine weeks.

Finding in plain language: More shared time was associated with greater closeness, including changes over time. Leisure activities and everyday conversation also mattered. Simply working in the same place is not equivalent to choosing to spend free time together.

Limits: Time and closeness were self-reported, and people choose to spend more time with someone they already like. Estimated hours are not universal thresholds or a guarantee of friendship.

For BWB: Record opportunities for contact and what partners actually do together before attributing a slow start to incompatibility. Discuss mutually acceptable ways to meet again; neither person owes increasing closeness in return for time invested. Distinguish how long people have known each other from time actually spent together.

Claims attributed to the sources #

The cards summarize associations with team performance and friendship closeness, alongside controlled evidence about teamwork training. Their findings concern the measured outcomes in the studied settings.

Evidence and access #

The collection contains five meta-analyses, including one systematic review of controlled interventions, and three primary observational papers. Six cards were checked against accessible full texts; de Jong and Selfhout are abstract-based. Access details and exact links appear in the search record.

Limits of the collection #

This purposeful starting selection does not establish the current consensus or completeness of any field. Its publications span 2003–2019. Primary datasets may recur within reviews; study counts cannot simply be added. Friendship evidence here is especially limited in population coverage.

What BWB takes forward #

Use trust, task demands, measured traits, knowledge coordination, contact opportunities, and training as comparison variables or conditions. Specify an outcome and test additional predictive value on new data.

What remains open #

Causal direction, durability, transfer between settings, and the relative roles of similarity and complementarity require further research. The cards do not resolve these questions universally.

Unsupported inferences and historical status #

Reject universal partner rankings, fixed friendship-hour thresholds, and claims of typology validation from these findings. These are historical contributions to an expanding literature; inclusion does not make an older result the final answer.
